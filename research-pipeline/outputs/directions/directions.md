## Direction 1: How Do Restaurant Subscriptions Affect Server Tip Income and Job Satisfaction? ⭐ TOP PICK

**Research Question**: Does adoption of a restaurant subscription program (e.g., an unlimited beverage subscription) reduce server tip income and job satisfaction, and does this effect vary by server characteristics (tenure, income dependence on tips)?

**Why it matters**:
- *Theoretical*: Links the subscription/sunk-cost literature to the emotional labor and tipping literatures for the first time. Contributes to the nascent "stakeholder welfare in platform pricing" conversation. Extends Iyengar et al. (2022) by measuring effects on a previously ignored stakeholder.
- *Practical*: Restaurant chains are adopting subscriptions with no evidence on labor consequences. Policy-relevant for minimum wage and tip credit debates. HR-relevant for server recruitment and retention.

**Feasibility**:
- Data needed: Server-level payroll + tip data from a restaurant chain before and after subscription launch; or a survey of servers at chains with/without subscriptions. Panera Bread has launched and expanded Sip Club — could be a natural experiment context if corporate data access is possible; alternatively, a server survey across chains is self-fundable.
- Method: Difference-in-differences (natural experiment) if chain-level data is accessible; or survey + mediation analysis (subscription exposure → tip norm change → tip income → job satisfaction)
- Estimated scope: 1 dissertation chapter (if natural experiment) or 1 standalone paper (if survey)

**Risk**:
- Corporate data from a chain is hard to obtain without a formal partnership. If survey-only, self-reported tip income has high variance. Reviewer concern: is the tip income effect large enough to matter if the beverage subscription doesn't change the dining experience (i.e., server still takes food orders)?

**Suggested journals**: Journal of Applied Psychology; Cornell Hospitality Quarterly; International Journal of Hospitality Management; Journal of Service Research

## Direction 2: Consumer vs. Employee Welfare Trade-offs in Restaurant Subscription Design

**Research Question**: Does the structure of a restaurant subscription (beverage-only vs. meal-inclusive vs. frequency-capped) create different trade-offs between consumer value maximization and employee wellbeing, and can a subscription design exist that optimizes both?

**Why it matters**:
- *Theoretical*: First paper to explicitly model the two-sided welfare consequences of restaurant pricing strategy. Bridges consumer behavior, operations management, and organizational behavior. Addresses the "tension point" identified in the gap analysis.
- *Practical*: Provides restaurant operators with a framework for subscription design that incorporates employee welfare constraints — relevant as labor relations become a brand concern.

**Feasibility**:
- Data needed: Multiple subscription types exist (Panera beverage, hypothetical meal plans). Could use conjoint analysis or vignette experiment with both consumers (WTP for different subscription structures) and servers (perceived fairness and workload of each structure).
- Method: Survey experiment / conjoint design; dual samples (consumer panel + server panel via Prolific or restaurant worker communities)
- Estimated scope: 1 standalone paper; could grow into a dissertation theme

**Risk**:
- "Dual-stakeholder" framing is harder to publish in single-discipline journals. Needs to be positioned carefully — either as a consumer behavior paper with employee welfare as a boundary condition, or as an OB paper with consumer behavior as context. Risk of reviewers saying it "falls between two stools."

**Suggested journals**: Management Science; Journal of Consumer Research; Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science; Journal of Service Research

## Direction 3: Do Subscription Members Behave Differently In-Visit? Within-Visit Consumption and Service Interaction Patterns

**Research Question**: Do restaurant subscription members differ from non-members in within-visit behavior (dwell time, order size, server interaction frequency, return rate), and do these behavioral differences affect server workload and tip income on a per-visit basis?

**Why it matters**:
- *Theoretical*: Decomposes the aggregate "subscription increases purchases" finding into within-visit vs. between-visit mechanisms. Directly tests whether the sunk cost effect manifests as more visits, longer visits, or higher spend per visit — each with different employee implications.
- *Practical*: Restaurant managers need to understand how subscriber behavior changes floor operations, staffing, and server section assignment.

**Feasibility**:
- Data needed: POS transaction data with subscriber vs. non-subscriber flag, table dwell time (from reservation system), server assignment. Requires a restaurant chain data partnership OR recruitment of individual restaurant owners.
- Method: Regression discontinuity or matching on subscriber status; within-server comparisons
- Estimated scope: 1 standalone paper; requires real transaction data (hardest to get)

**Risk**:
- Data access is the primary barrier. Without a corporate data partnership, this cannot be executed. High payoff if data is available; not feasible otherwise.

**Suggested journals**: Marketing Science; Journal of Marketing Research; Management Science

## Direction 4: The Sunk Cost Effect in Food Subscriptions — Heterogeneity by Consumption Self-Control

**Research Question**: Does the sunk cost effect of a restaurant subscription increase visit frequency more for consumers with lower self-control, and do these consumers report greater well-being (sense of commitment) or regret (overconsumption)?

**Why it matters**:
- *Theoretical*: Zhang et al. (2024) model predicts consumers strategically use subscriptions as a self-control commitment device. This is the first empirical test of this mechanism in a restaurant context. Extends the sunk cost literature by examining heterogeneity by self-control capacity.
- *Practical*: Has implications for consumer welfare — if low-self-control consumers are disproportionately driven to overconsume, this is a potential dark side of restaurant subscriptions.

**Feasibility**:
- Data needed: Behavioral self-control measure (Brief Self-Control Scale) + stated visit frequency before/after subscription sign-up; or experiment with simulated subscription.
- Method: Online survey experiment with subscription enrollment simulation; moderation by self-control; outcomes are intended visit frequency, regret, perceived value
- Estimated scope: 1 standalone paper; fully self-fundable via MTurk/Prolific (~$500–1,000)

**Risk**:
- Zhang et al. (2024) already makes this prediction theoretically; if a reviewer says "we already know this from the model," the empirical contribution needs to be emphasized strongly. The restaurant / food context is genuinely new. Reviewers may want more than one study.

**Suggested journals**: Journal of Consumer Psychology; Journal of Marketing Research; Journal of Consumer Research

## Direction 5: Server Emotional Labor Under Subscription-Induced Repeat Customer Relationships

**Research Question**: Does serving the same subscription customers repeatedly (vs. one-time customers) reduce or increase server emotional labor demands and burnout, and does this vary by the quality of the server–customer relationship?

**Why it matters**:
- *Theoretical*: Directly tests the competing predictions of Cayla & Auriacombe (2025) [positive energy from regulars] vs. JD-R model [customer contact → burnout]. The subscription setting is a natural moderator of customer regularity not previously exploited in the emotional labor literature.
- *Practical*: If regular subscription customers are net positive for server wellbeing, restaurants have an argument that subscriptions improve labor conditions. If negative, the opposite.

**Feasibility**:
- Data needed: Server diary study or experience sampling; or cross-sectional survey comparing servers at subscription vs. non-subscription restaurants on burnout (MBI) and emotional exhaustion measures.
- Method: Experience sampling method (ESM) over 4–6 weeks capturing daily interactions and emotional outcomes; or cross-sectional survey
- Estimated scope: 1 dissertation chapter or 1 standalone paper

**Risk**:
- Defining "subscription customer" from the server's perspective is operationally tricky (servers don't always know who has a subscription). May need to proxy with "recognized regular customer." ESM studies require participant commitment and have high attrition.

**Suggested journals**: Journal of Applied Psychology; Journal of Organizational Behavior; International Journal of Hospitality Management
